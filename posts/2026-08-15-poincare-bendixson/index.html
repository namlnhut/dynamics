The Poincaré–Bendixson theorem

planar-systems
limit-cycles
In the plane, a trajectory trapped away from equilibria has nowhere to go but a closed orbit. A tool for proving limit cycles exist.
Author

Nam Le

Published

15 August 2026

Equilibria are found by solving \(f(x) = 0\). Periodic orbits are much harder to find, since they are global objects. In the plane there is a clean topological criterion for their existence.

Theorem 1 (Poincaré–Bendixson) Let \(f\) be \(C^1\) on an open set containing a closed bounded region \(R \subset \R^2\), and suppose \(R\) contains no equilibria of \(\dot x = f(x)\). If some trajectory starts in \(R\) and stays in \(R\) for all \(t \geq 0\), then it is a closed orbit or it spirals toward a closed orbit as \(t \to \infty\). In either case \(R\) contains a closed orbit.

The proof rests on the Jordan curve theorem: a closed curve splits the plane into an inside and an outside, so a planar trajectory cannot weave around itself the way it can in three dimensions (Perko 2001, sec. 3.7). In practice the theorem is applied through trapping regions.

Corollary 1 (Trapping regions) Let \(R\) be a closed annulus containing no equilibria. If the vector field points into \(R\) everywhere on its boundary, then \(R\) contains a closed orbit.

Proof. Any trajectory starting in \(R\) can never leave it, so Theorem 1 applies.

A worked example

Example 1 Following Strogatz (2015, sec. 7.3), consider in polar coordinates \[ \dot r = r\,(1 - r^2 + \mu\cos\theta), \qquad \dot\theta = 1, \tag{1}\] with \(0 < \mu < 1\). There are no equilibria except the origin, since \(\dot\theta = 1\). On a circle \(r = r_{\min}\) with \(r_{\min}^2 < 1 - \mu\), \[ 1 - r_{\min}^2 + \mu\cos\theta \geq 1 - r_{\min}^2 - \mu > 0, \] so \(\dot r > 0\) and the flow points outward. On a circle \(r = r_{\max}\) with \(r_{\max}^2 > 1 + \mu\), the same bound in the other direction gives \(\dot r < 0\). The annulus \(r_{\min} \leq r \leq r_{\max}\) is therefore a trapping region, and by Corollary 1 it contains a closed orbit.

Figure 1 shows the result for \(\mu = 0.5\): trajectories from inside and outside are drawn to a single closed orbit lying between the circles \(r = \sqrt{1 - \mu}\) and \(r = \sqrt{1 + \mu}\).

Code 
mu = 0.5

def field(x):
    r = np.hypot(x[0], x[1])
    g = 1 - r**2 + (mu * x[0] / r if r > 0 else 0.0)
    return np.array([x[0] * g - x[1], x[1] * g + x[0]])

g = np.linspace(-1.8, 1.8, 40)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(g, g)
R = np.hypot(X, Y)
G = 1 - R**2 + mu * np.divide(X, R, out=np.zeros_like(X), where=R > 0)
U, W = X * G - Y, Y * G + X

_, inner = rk4(field, [0.15, 0.0], 12, h=0.01)
_, outer = rk4(field, [1.75, 0.0], 12, h=0.01)
_, cycle = rk4(field, inner[-1], 2 * np.pi, h=0.01)
phi = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 200)

def draw(fig, ax, c):
    ax.streamplot(X, Y, U, W, color=c.muted, density=0.8, linewidth=0.5, arrowsize=0.7)
    for rad in (np.sqrt(1 - mu), np.sqrt(1 + mu)):
        ax.plot(rad * np.cos(phi), rad * np.sin(phi), "--", color=c.fg, lw=1)
    ax.plot(inner[:, 0], inner[:, 1], color=c.accent2, lw=1)
    ax.plot(outer[:, 0], outer[:, 1], color=c.accent2, lw=1)
    ax.plot(cycle[:, 0], cycle[:, 1], color=c.accent, lw=2.2)
    ax.set(xlim=(-1.8, 1.8), ylim=(-1.8, 1.8), aspect="equal", xlabel=r"$x$", ylabel=r"$y$")

themed(draw, figsize=(5.5, 5.5))

The van der Pol oscillator

The same idea, with a more careful choice of region, proves that the van der Pol oscillator \[ \ddot x - \mu\,(1 - x^2)\,\dot x + x = 0, \qquad \mu > 0, \] has a periodic orbit. Liénard’s theorem goes further and shows the orbit is unique and attracts every trajectory except the equilibrium at the origin (Perko 2001, sec. 3.8). Figure 2 shows how the cycle changes shape: nearly circular for small \(\mu\), and a relaxation oscillation with slow drifts and fast jumps for large \(\mu\).

Code 
def vdp(mu):
    f = lambda x: np.array([x[1], mu * (1 - x[0]**2) * x[1] - x[0]])
    _, xs = rk4(f, [0.5, 0.0], 60, h=0.005)
    return xs[len(xs) // 2:]

cycles = {m: vdp(m) for m in (0.3, 1.0, 3.0)}

def draw(fig, ax, c):
    for (m, xs), col in zip(cycles.items(), (c.accent, c.accent2, c.fg)):
        ax.plot(xs[:, 0], xs[:, 1], color=col, lw=1.4, label=fr"$\mu = {m:g}$")
    ax.set(xlabel=r"$x$", ylabel=r"$\dot x$")
    ax.legend(loc="upper left")

themed(draw, figsize=(7, 4.2))

Remark. Theorem 1 is also the reason chaos needs at least three dimensions: in the plane, a bounded trajectory away from equilibria can only settle onto a closed orbit. The Lorenz system escapes this by living in \(\R^3\).

## The van der Pol oscillator

The same idea, with a more careful choice of region, proves that the van der Pol oscillator
$$
\ddot x - \mu\,(1 - x^2)\,\dot x + x = 0, \qquad \mu > 0,
$$
has a periodic orbit. Liénard's theorem goes further and shows the orbit is unique and attracts every trajectory except the equilibrium at the origin [@perko2001, sec. 3.8]. @fig-vdp shows how the cycle changes shape: nearly circular for small $\mu$, and a *relaxation oscillation* with slow drifts and fast jumps for large $\mu$.

::: {#fig-vdp}
```{python}
#| renderings: [light, dark]
#| fig-alt: "Limit cycles of the van der Pol oscillator for mu equal to 0.3, 1 and 3, becoming less circular as mu grows."
def vdp(mu):
    f = lambda x: np.array([x[1], mu * (1 - x[0]**2) * x[1] - x[0]])

def draw(fig, ax, c):
```
